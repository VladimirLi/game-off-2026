# What makes insight / "thinky" puzzle games compelling: research notes

Compiled 2026-11-12. Every source listed below was opened. Where a page could not be opened (403, paywall, empty), the claim is marked **[UNVERIFIED]** and the page is not listed as a source.

## 1. Designers on what makes a good puzzle and a good set

### Jonathan Blow (Braid, The Witness)
- **Start from the "truth" of a mechanic and explore it fully.** IndieCade 2011: to design a good puzzle, set up a situation, look at what is true about it, and build from that. A design built on a truth is worth something even when the execution is imperfect, "because the truth is valuable." He tests a rule against every object in the world, and the line-can't-cross-itself rule "came from exploration, not from a top-down decision." (Kumar, 2011)
- **Surprise that is still consistent is the content.** "If you send someone a message and it only says exactly what they expected to get from you, then there's no information in that message." (Kumar, 2011)
- **Find truths, don't invent them.** GDC Europe 2011: "I don't know how to concoct truth." He urges designers "to refuse to be satisfied with the first level of ideas" and says "Contemporary game design is so often about dictating, rather than listening." (Parkin, 2011)
- **Clarity amplifies epiphany.** "The clearer and simpler the puzzle is, the more beautiful and strong that feeling of epiphany can be." Also: "I like games that ask something of the player, that have faith the player is a smart person." (Alexander, 2014)
- [UNVERIFIED] GMTK's "How Jonathan Blow Designs a Puzzle" (2016) argues that a puzzle is "a communication from the designer to the player about the game's universe". That line comes from the episode description on a notes page; the transcript (Amara) returned 403. The common summary that the video says to strip out noise and show why the obvious approach fails could not be checked.

### Arvi Teikari (Baba Is You)
- **Work backwards from a cool interaction.** He designs by "thinking of cool or amusing combinations/interactions between the words, and then working backwards." He likes puzzles that are "hard to wrap one's head around but that are ultimately simple." (Couture, 2018)
- **Unintended solutions.** "Playtesting has shown that it's extremely easy to be blind to certain types of unintended solutions." He blocks them, or keeps one if it is "interesting enough." (Couture, 2018) In 2019: "almost every single level I made had at least one alternative way to solve the level", and "there are some forms of alternative solutions that trivialise the level". (Dealessandri, 2019)
- **Solo designers misjudge difficulty.** "it's very easy for that designer to become blind to a lot of the difficulty." He felt he "failed in a sense, as the game is still considered extremely difficult." (Dealessandri, 2019)
- He cites Snakebird among the "really good turn-based, block pushing-based puzzle games" he played before Baba Is You. (Dealessandri, 2019)

### Alan Hazelden (Cosmic Express, A Monster's Expedition)
- **Each puzzle is built around one required interaction.** His process starts by "choosing a single interaction, or chain of interactions, that I want you to have to do as part of the solution." He calls iteration "essentially it's a game of whack a mole" (closing off shortcuts). On the open-world structure: "solving a puzzle on one island gives you access to another island with a different puzzle." (Couture, 2021)
- **Fairness means the rule is observable.** A playtester "took five minutes to realise in level 2 that the train can only carry one passenger at a time." Hazelden's response: "this is not unfair, you just have to pay attention to what's happening". On players who quit that early: they "probably wouldn't have enjoyed the rest of the game anyway." (Goodwin, 2018)
- [UNVERIFIED] His TouchArcade 2024 interview (403) reportedly says Draknek's future games will ship with hint systems at launch.

### Patrick Traynor (Patrick's Parabox), a strong comparison
- "make the easiest possible version of a puzzle, that still features the interesting idea or point."
- "Prefer the behavior that makes more interesting puzzles."
- "I enjoy easy, flow-y puzzles that still have interesting ideas."
- "I was okay with people totally stumbling upon a solution and not understanding it at all", provided later puzzles reinforce the idea.
- "I was pretty ruthless when it came to iterating on puzzles". He borrowed an "Unlock all puzzles" option from Cosmic Express. (Harris, 2023)

### Stephen Lavelle / increpare (Stephen's Sausage Roll, PuzzleScript)
- A 2012 profile records him calling his early English Country Tune levels "the worst sort of game I can imagine" and "really unbearable". In other words, he is willing to throw out levels that are clever but unpleasant to play. (Salgado, 2012)
- Goodwin (2018) describes SSR as having "almost certainly no holding of hands" and says "There is no notion of practice": every level is new content, not drill.
- [UNVERIFIED] I found no direct Lavelle interview on SSR's level philosophy. The RPS 2016 article came up only as a citation.

### Portal (Valve)
- From the developer commentary: Robin Walker says "Portal is effectively an extended player training exercise" and "we very deliberately introduce and train each gameplay concept". Kim Swift: "Overwhelmed players tend not to digest new information." Chris Chin: "we still ended up introducing some concepts too quickly." Kerry Davis: "Playtesters often assumed that orange portals were exit-only", so the team built a chamber that forces players through an orange portal. Garret Rickey: "Portal momentum ended up being the hardest concept to convey." (Portal Wiki transcription)
- GDC 2008 postmortem: Swift called playtesting "probably the most important thing we did on Portal": "Sit down and watch people play your game." A shimmering force field that confused players was turned into glass, and the world was cleaned up so players could read it. (Kumar, 2008)
- Mark Brown on Valve: test early (within days) and often (weekly), stay silent while watching, and treat feedback as data rather than orders. Mike Ambinder: "We see our game designs as hypotheses and our playtests as experiments". (Brown, 2023)

### Zach Gage
- "A game has a responsibility to educate its players and turn low-skill players into high-skill players." "The game should be healthy." On Pile-Up Poker's session cap of five hands: "five hands is even more than I am sort of into... I would like it to be three." Also: "we are not building a poker game that can ruin your life." (Snell, 2024)

### Snakebird (Noumenon)
- The dev-history post (Eliasson, 2015) shows an early node-based world map. The first level designed survived almost unchanged as level 9.
- [UNVERIFIED] Eurogamer praising the "nonlinear level selection map" and TIGSource praising the undo feature appear only in search snippets and Wikipedia-style summaries, which I did not open.

## 2. Concepts

- **The "aha" moment, and clarity.** Blow (Alexander, 2014) says the simpler the puzzle, the stronger the epiphany. Teikari prefers "hard to wrap one's head around but ... ultimately simple." Hermans (2018): "minimalist is the way to go to respect the player's time".
- **Red herrings vs fairness.** Hermans (2018) defines a red herring as "a puzzle element that seems relevant, but isn't required" and treats it as clutter. He suggests making seemingly useless pieces matter in unexpected ways instead. Siggy (2026): "red herrings often dominate the solving experience, while being completely invisible to the designer", "puzzle designer intuitions are frequently wrong", and "difficult puzzles requires some degree of trust." Hazelden's test for fairness: is the rule visible to an attentive player? (Goodwin, 2018)
- **False solutions / traps.** In Blow's framing, surprise only carries information if it was not expected (Kumar, 2011). The deliberate "near-miss" solution design is described in the Thinky Games misdirection essay [UNVERIFIED, 403]. Teikari blocks alternative solutions that "trivialise the level".
- **One idea per level.** Hazelden: one required interaction per puzzle. Traynor: the easiest version that still holds the idea. Hermans: "If the designer wants to keep teaching you new things in every levels, puzzles become much more focused and elegant." Portal: each concept is introduced and trained deliberately, and a chamber was cut for "too many new concepts at once" (commentary, per search summary).
- **Teaching without text.** Portal shapes the environment around wrong assumptions players actually made in testing, such as forcing entry through an orange portal. Blow: "we don't have to shine spotlights on things, because they don't stand out unless we mean for them to stand out." Hermans: the visuals must explain how things behave. He calls this "the single most important function of art in a puzzle game." Josh Singer (Tricone Lab) deliberately slowed the first levels; the first "true puzzle" is level 9 (Goodwin, 2018 comments).
- **Difficulty curve and sawtooth.** Grannell (2012) relays Christopher Phin's sawtooth model: each set gets harder, then eases off when the next set starts. It gives "spice and breathing space." Portal raises the share of time-pressured puzzles toward the end (commentary). Gage's Good Sudoku runs a weekly ramp that ends in a hard Sunday puzzle (search snippet only, [UNVERIFIED]).
- **Level select vs linear.** Hazelden's archipelago uses non-linear gating. Traynor and Cosmic Express offer "Unlock all puzzles". Snakebird uses a node map. Non-linearity lets a stuck player switch to another puzzle instead of quitting. (This is inference, not a measured result.)
- **Optional or bonus levels.** No primary source opened on this. Common practice (Parabox, Baba, A Monster's Expedition) puts the hardest content off the critical path. [UNVERIFIED as stated design intent]
- **Undo and restart.** Ernest Adams (2003): "there's no fundamental reason to penalize the player for mistakes in a puzzle game". Unlimited undo/redo encourages experimentation, and without it "you'll lose players like me". He suggests disabling undo only in an optional "nightmare mode".
- **A minimal ruleset with deep consequences.** Blow: explore one mechanic deeply and "refuse to be satisfied with the first level of ideas." Traynor: choose whichever rule behavior yields more interesting puzzles. Teikari: start from an emergent interaction between rules and work backwards.
- **Rule discovery.** Goodwin (2018) notes a design trend "where the player has to figure out the basics". Hazelden's position is that hidden-but-observable rules are fair. Traynor accepts accidental solves when later puzzles force the understanding.

## 3. Game feel and juice for turn-based games
- **Juice It or Lose It** (Jonasson & Purho, GDC Europe 2012, Indie Games Summit). Per the official abstract, they "demonstrate the neat little tricks you can apply to any game to make it more satisfying to play", turning up a plain Breakout clone live with particles, screen shake, sound and tweening.
- **Counterpoint: Folmer Kelly, "Don't Juice It or Lose It"** (GDC Europe 2014). Polish makes a game feel alive, but "we're actually losing a level of immersion" when effects are disconnected from context.
- **Steve Swink, "Game Feel: The Secret Ingredient"** (Gamasutra, 2007). Game feel is "The tactile sensation of manipulating a digital agent". His components are input, response, context and polish. Polish exists "to convey the physical properties of objects through their motion and interaction." His definition is about real-time control, so turn-based puzzles mostly get the polish layer, not the response layer.
- **Turn-based practice.** Krystman (Pico-8 chess, 2018): "Slow animations would make the game feel sluggish and unresponsive"; "Quick, sudden movements feel dynamic". He warns against slowing pieces down to show off animation. Dan Prince (grid roguelike, 2020): update the logic state immediately, then tween the sprite. Watch for input arriving mid-animation. Playtesters preferred the tweened version "far more".
- **Hazelden's team** (Couture, 2021): every system should either remove friction from interacting with puzzles or provide rest. The music quiets while you think (per search summary; the article attributes this to Benjamin Davis).
- **How much juice a puzzle needs (synthesis):** fast, snappy moves (around 0.1 to 0.15 s, an [UNVERIFIED] rule of thumb), animations that undo and restart can interrupt, clear feedback on blocked moves, and one larger payoff on level complete. Never let juice slow down thinking or input.

## 4. Session structure and the "one more level" loop
- Short, self-contained levels with instant restart and undo keep the cost of starting another level near zero (Adams 2003; Traynor's "easy, flow-y" puzzles).
- Non-linear gating gives a stuck player somewhere else to go (Hazelden, 2021).
- Ending well: Gage deliberately caps sessions ("I would like it to be three") and refuses infinite-engagement patterns (Snell, 2024). For a jam game, a natural stopping point at the end of a world or chapter, plus a visible next goal, is the respectful version of "one more level".
- No hard data found on session length for premium puzzle games. [UNVERIFIED]

## 5. Why players quit
- **Walls early on.** Hadley (GameAnalytics, 2025): a first puzzle that is too hard "can cause players to leave the game". Players who forget a long-unused mechanic "either google it, or just give up". Paid skips make players "less likely to rage quit". These are the author's observations; there is no data.
- **General completion data, not puzzle-specific.** Bycer (2020): "Most people are not going to finish your game no matter how much praise it gets." Hollow Knight lost 26% of players before the first charm, and Dark Souls 3 lost 18% at the first boss. Steep early drop-off is normal.
- **Mobile puzzle data.** Korbut (SayGames, 2026): even with an average of two starts per level, some players need "10 or even 20 attempts to win". "What looks like churn can simply be a pause." No hard churn numbers are given.
- **Designer blindness to difficulty** is the root cause behind several of these: Teikari, Siggy, and Grannell ("Difficulty walls have long been a problem in gaming").
- **Unclear rules.** Portal's playtest fixes (force field turned to glass, orange-portal chamber), and Hazelden's line that a rule should be observable.
- **Busywork.** Hermans: minimal levels respect the player's time. Lavelle discarded levels he found "unbearable".
